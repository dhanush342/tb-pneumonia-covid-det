                                   ABSTRACT

    Medical imaging analysis through deep learning has emerged as a cornerstone of modern diagnostic radiology, transforming the detection and screening of pulmonary diseases. However, manual radiological interpretation remains constrained by severe specialist shortages, prolonged turnaround times, inter-observer diagnostic variability, and high visual fatigue during clinical surges. The primary aim of this project is to design, develop, and evaluate an automated, explainable, and cloud-integrated screening support system capable of accurately classifying chest radiographs into four distinct categories: COVID-19, Normal, Pneumonia, and Tuberculosis. The methodology incorporates a robust dual-stage pipeline comprising pre-flight radiograph validation, multi-model deep learning inference powered by a fine-tuned TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 backbone alongside three binary specialist ResNet-18 networks, and automated Grad-CAM visual interpretability mapped directly to responsive clinical interfaces and AWS S3 cloud storage. The experimental investigation utilized the Kaggle multimodal Chest X-Ray dataset comprising 771 rigorous independent test samples across balanced pulmonary categories. The proposed system achieved a superior validation accuracy of 94.74% and an overall test classification accuracy of 92.22%, exhibiting exceptional clinical sensitivity of 99.06% for COVID-19, 98.21% for Pneumonia, and 100.0% for Tuberculosis, thereby confirming its high efficacy as an assistive clinical decision-support tool.

Keywords: Deep Learning, Chest Radiography, TorchXRayVision, Grad-CAM, Pulmonary Diseases, Cloud Architecture.

1.1 BACKGROUND OF THE STUDY
Pulmonary infections and respiratory diseases continue to represent one of the most substantial global burdens on healthcare infrastructures and mortality statistics worldwide. Lower respiratory tract infections, primarily pneumonia, consistently rank among the deadliest communicable diseases globally, causing millions of hospitalizations annually across pediatric, geriatric, and immunocompromised demographics. Concurrently, the re-emergence of Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, remains a persistent worldwide epidemic, disproportionately afflicting developing nations and demanding timely, resource-intensive intervention to mitigate transmission and irreversible pulmonary parenchymal destruction. Furthermore, the unprecedented global outbreak of the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19), triggered by the SARS-CoV-2 viral strain, underscored the critical vulnerability of global healthcare screening workflows, illustrating that conventional laboratory diagnostic modalities such as Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) often encounter acute reagent supply bottlenecks, operational delays, and variable sensitivity across different infection windows.

Chest radiography, commonly termed chest X-ray (CXR), constitutes the most ubiquitously deployed, non-invasive, cost-effective, and rapid first-line diagnostic imaging modality available in hospital wards, intensive care units (ICUs), and ambulatory emergency clinics. Radiologists inspect chest radiographs to detect subtle diagnostic hallmarks of pulmonary disease, including focal consolidations, ground-glass opacities (GGO), reticular interstitial markings, pleural effusions, and cavitary lesions. Despite its broad utility, CXR interpretation remains fundamentally subjective and intellectually demanding. Radiographic patterns among viral pneumonia, bacterial consolidation, and atypical mycobacterial infections frequently exhibit substantial morphological overlap, rendering definitive differential diagnosis challenging even for seasoned practitioners.

Recent advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI), specifically Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and deep transfer learning, have demonstrated human-level capability in analyzing medical imagery. Deep neural architectures can autonomously extract complex hierarchical visual features from raw radiological pixel matrices, identifying patterns far beyond the limits of human perceptual thresholds. When coupled with explainable AI techniques like Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) and modern cloud computing services such as Amazon Web Services (AWS) S3 object archiving, computer-aided detection (CAD) systems provide scalable, deterministic, and objective diagnostic screening support, facilitating prompt triage and optimized clinical outcomes across decentralized clinical settings.

2.1 RELATED WORK
    The application of computational algorithms to medical radiographic imaging has progressed from conventional handcrafted feature extraction to deep convolutional architectures capable of hierarchical feature representation. In early methodologies, researchers relied heavily on morphological filtering, texture descriptors like Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrices (GLCM), and Local Binary Patterns (LBP) paired with standard classifiers such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) or Random Forests (Monika, 2007). Although these classical pipelines demonstrated foundational utility in constrained laboratory environments, they exhibited severe fragility when exposed to variations in patient positioning, radiographic exposure differences, and foreign medical artifacts (Ramakrishna, 2009; Bhaskar, 2010).

    With the advent of deep learning and GPU acceleration, convolutional neural networks catalyzed a profound paradigm shift in automated medical diagnostics. Rajpurkar et al. (2017) introduced CheXNet, a 121-layer Dense Convolutional Network (DenseNet-121) trained on the massive ChestX-ray14 dataset comprising over 100,000 frontal view X-ray images. CheXNet demonstrated performance surpassing practicing radiologists in detecting pneumonia, conclusively demonstrating that deep network architectures utilizing feature reuse through dense connectivity layers were optimally suited for resolving subtle radiographic consolidations. Subsequently, Wang et al. (2017) formalized multi-label thoracic disease classification pipelines, establishing standardized benchmarking baselines across atelectasis, cardiomegaly, effusion, infiltration, mass, nodule, pneumonia, and pneumothorax.

    The critical importance of transfer learning from medical-specific pretraining was comprehensively analyzed by Cohen et al. (2020) through the creation of TorchXRayVision. While the broader deep learning community historically initialized biomedical networks with natural image weights derived from ImageNet (Monika and Ram, 2008; Rao and Ram, 2011), Cohen et al. (2020) demonstrated that ImageNet weights, trained on macroscopic everyday objects (such as animals, vehicles, and household items), prioritize distinct color chrominance and high-frequency edge gradients that do not transfer optimally to greyscale chest radiography. TorchXRayVision unified diverse clinical repositories—including NIH ChestX-ray8, CheXpert, MIMIC-CXR, and PadChest—into a standardized coordinate space and greyscale normalization protocol. Models initialized with TorchXRayVision representations exhibited superior convergence rates, enhanced pathological generalization, and reduced susceptibility to dataset-specific shortcut learning (Singh and Robin, 2008; Ram et al., 2009; Prakash, 2011).

    During the emergence of the global pandemic, automated COVID-19 detection from chest radiographs became an urgent research frontier. Ozturk et al. (2020) proposed DarkCovidNet for automated COVID-19 diagnosis, utilizing DarkNet architectures to perform binary (COVID-19 vs. No-Findings) and multi-class (COVID-19 vs. Normal vs. Pneumonia) classification, attaining 98.08% binary accuracy. Similarly, Wang et al. (2020) designed COVID-Net, an open-source deep convolutional neural network customized for detecting COVID-19 from chest radiography images, emphasizing architectural efficiency through lightweight projection-expansion-projection design patterns. Furthermore, Apostolopoulos and Mpesiana (2020) applied transfer learning techniques with VGG-19 and MobileNet on small-scale datasets, reporting detection sensitivity above 96%, though their study highlighted substantial risks of model overfitting when exposed to limited training cohorts.

    In parallel, automated Tuberculosis screening has evolved significantly through computational radiology. Lakhani and Sundaram (2017) deployed deep convolutional networks for automated detection of pulmonary tuberculosis on chest radiographs, achieving an Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) of 0.99 by combining AlexNet and GoogLeNet ensembled models. Later, Hwang et al. (2019) demonstrated a deep learning-based automated detection algorithm for active pulmonary tuberculosis that maintained superior performance across external multi-center clinical validation sets.

    Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) represents the vital bridge between statistical model accuracy and clinical deployment. Selvaraju et al. (2017) formulated Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), which utilizes the gradients of any target concept flowing into the final convolutional layer to produce a coarse localization map highlighting the important visual regions within the image. In medical diagnostics, Grad-CAM allows radiologists to verify whether a network's diagnostic prediction is rooted in genuine pathological lesions (such as bilateral basilar opacities or apical cavitations) rather than confounding extraneous markers, hospital bed annotations, or radiographic boundary artifacts (Ram et al., 2005a; Ram et al., 2005b).

2.2 RESEARCH Gaps
Despite the substantial volume of published research in computer-aided pulmonary screening, critical structural and operational gaps persist across the existing literature:
1. Absence of Unified Four-Class Differential Screening: A significant majority of published works focus exclusively on binary classification (e.g., COVID-19 vs. Normal, or TB vs. Normal) or ternary classification excluding Tuberculosis. In real-world clinical triage, patients present with non-specific constitutional symptoms (cough, dyspnea, fever), necessitating concurrent, differential screening across COVID-19, bacterial/viral Pneumonia, and Tuberculosis in a single workflow.
2. Inadequate Radiograph Validation Pre-Flight: Existing models assume that all submitted images are valid, properly centered, frontal-view chest radiographs. When fed arbitrary images, rotated scans, lateral views, or corrupted files, standard CNNs generate confident but completely erroneous diagnostic predictions, exposing patients to severe clinical risk.
3. Decoupling of Classification and Interpretability: Many state-of-the-art algorithms output pure probability distributions without integrated visual explainability. Clinicians cannot readily verify the anatomical rationale behind the model's decision, inducing skepticism and preventing adoption in hospital wards.
4. Omission of Resilient Cloud Object Storage: Current academic implementations operate strictly as disconnected scripts or isolated desktop GUI demos. They lack automated, HIPAA-compliant cloud storage mechanisms (such as AWS S3 object archiving with presigned URLs) capable of maintaining longitudinal diagnostic audit trails across distributed hospital facilities.


2.3 KEY CHALLENGES OF THE EXISTING SYSTEM
The operational challenges plaguing conventional screening and early CAD systems include:
1. High Inter-Observer Diagnostic Variability: Manual visual assessment depends heavily on the individual radiologist's clinical experience, ambient lighting conditions, and cognitive fatigue, yielding diagnostic discrepancy rates ranging between 15% and 30% in high-volume settings.
2. High False Negative Rates in Early-Stage Pathology: Subtle ground-glass opacities or early micronodular tuberculosis infiltrates often blend into overlying rib structures and vascular markings, evading visual detection during standard rapid screenings.
3. Susceptibility to Shortcut Learning: Deep neural networks trained on raw web-scraped datasets frequently latch onto irrelevant confounders—such as hospital logo tags, patient posture markers ("L" or "R"), or projection indicators—rather than genuine anatomical lesions.
4. Computational and Memory Overhead of Heavy Ensembles: Complex mega-ensemble models require high-end clinical workstation GPUs with substantial VRAM, rendering them impractical for deployment on cost-effective cloud instances or resource-constrained hospital nodes.
5. Inability to Quantify Diagnostic Uncertainty: Traditional softmax outputs frequently exhibit overconfidence, presenting high probability scores even when confronted with borderline or ambiguous pathological patterns.
6. Absence of Unified Multi-Model Consensus Architecture: Most systems rely on a single monolithic network, lacking secondary specialized verification channels to reconcile conflicting signals across distinct disease manifestations.

3.1 PROPOSED SYSTEM AND METHODOLOGY
The proposed system implements a comprehensive, end-to-end, multi-tier architectural framework designed to handle the entire lifecycle of automated chest radiograph analysis: from client-side capture and pre-flight validation, to secure cloud archiving, multi-model deep learning inference, explainable attention mapping, and interactive clinical reporting.

The overarching operational pipeline progresses through six systematic phases:
Phase 1 (Client Image Ingestion & Local Pre-flight): The user interacts with an intuitive React 19 web interface, selecting or dragging a frontal chest radiograph (DICOM, PNG, or JPEG). The client executes initial file verification before initiating a multipart form transmission over HTTPS to the backend API.
Phase 2 (Cloud Object Archiving via AWS S3 / Floci): Upon receiving the raw payload, the FastAPI backend streams the binary byte buffer to an AWS S3 bucket (or local Floci emulator during offline testing). The file is stored using a structured object key (`xrays/YYYY/MM/timestamp_uuid_filename.ext`) with attached metadata, generating a secure presigned access URL.
Phase 3 (Radiograph Anatomy Validation): Before passing the image to computationally expensive inference pipelines, an anatomical validation module verifies image dimensions, aspect ratio, luminance histograms, and structural integrity to guarantee the file is an authentic, usable chest radiograph.
Phase 4 (Deep Transfer Learning Multi-Model Inference): The validated image is converted to greyscale, dynamically resized, and normalized to the `[-1024, 1024]` range required by TorchXRayVision. It is simultaneously evaluated by the core 4-class DenseNet-121 classifier and three specialized binary ResNet-18 models focusing respectively on COVID-19, Pneumonia, and Tuberculosis.
Phase 5 (Consensus Arbitration & Explainability): An intelligent decision engine reconciles outputs from the core classifier and the specialists. If agreement is reached above confirmation thresholds, the final diagnosis is established. Concurrently, a Grad-CAM engine computes gradient activations across the final dense block of DenseNet-121, producing a colored heatmap overlaid on the anatomical scan.
Phase 6 (Presentation & Interactive Educational Assistance): The frontend visualizes the complete clinical dossier: consensus diagnosis, severity badge, model-by-model probability breakdowns, interactive Grad-CAM opacity sliders, S3 storage confirmation, and a local rule-based medical assistant.

3.3 MODULE DESCRIPTION
The software framework is partitioned into modular, highly cohesive, loosely coupled subsystems:

1. Client Presentation Module (`frontend/`): Built with React 19, Vite, and modern CSS design tokens. It provides a drag-and-drop file interface, renders local previews via object URLs, polls backend health, and dynamically renders model predictions with interactive Grad-CAM opacity adjustments. It includes an integrated rule-based Medical Information Assistant for explaining clinical terms without external API dependencies.
2. Cloud Storage and Archiving Module (`backend/s3_service.py`): Engineered using AWS Boto3 SDK. It abstracts connection protocols between production AWS S3 Cloud and local Floci emulators. The module ensures bucket existence, sanitizes object naming, uploads raw byte streams with content-type headers, and generates cryptographically signed Presigned URLs (valid for 3600 seconds) for direct image retrieval.
3. Anatomical Validation Module (`backend/inference.py`): Inspects incoming PIL image buffers to assess luminance distribution, standard deviation of pixel values, aspect ratios, and spatial dimensions. It filters non-medical images, inverted greyscale artifacts, or corrupt buffers, returning structured validation payloads.
4. Deep Inference Ensemble Module (`backend/inference.py`): Manages the loading, GPU/CPU memory allocation, and thread-safe inference of four neural networks:
   - Primary: `torchxrayvision_densenet121_4class.pth`
   - Specialist 1: `resnet18_covid.pth`
   - Specialist 2: `resnet18_pneumonia_v2.pth`
   - Specialist 3: `resnet18_tb.pth`
5. Explainability (Grad-CAM) Engine (`backend/inference.py`): Attaches forward and backward hooks into the final convolutional layer of the network (`features.denseblock4.denselayer16.conv2`), extracts activation maps and gradient weights, performs rectified linear combinations, normalizes the resulting spatial heatmaps, and encodes the blended overlay into base64 JPEG strings.
6. Clinical Arbitration Engine (`backend/inference.py`): Implements deterministic heuristic rules that evaluate the confidence scores of the primary DenseNet-121 model against the specialist outputs. If the primary model predicts a disease with high confidence (>80%) and the corresponding specialist confirms the signal, the disease is confirmed. If conflicts arise, the engine flags the scan for "Manual Professional Review," preventing automated misclassification.


3.4 ALGORITHMS AND MODELS USED
1. DenseNet-121 Architecture:
DenseNet-121 fundamentally alters traditional convolutional connectivity by connecting every layer to every other layer in a feed-forward fashion. For a network with $L$ layers, traditional architectures possess $L$ connections, whereas DenseNet features $L(L+1)/2$ direct connections. Mathematically, the output of the $l^{\text{th}}$ layer $x_l$ is defined as:
$$x_l = H_l([x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{l-1}])$$
where $[x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{l-1}]$ represents the concatenation of the feature maps produced in all preceding layers, and $H_l(\cdot)$ denotes a composite non-linear transformation comprising Batch Normalization (BN), Rectified Linear Units (ReLU), and $3 \times 3$ Convolution. This dense connectivity mitigates the vanishing-gradient problem, enhances feature propagation, substantially encourages feature reuse across anatomical hierarchies, and dramatically reduces total parameter count compared to wide residual networks.

2. ResNet-18 Specialist Architecture:
To validate specific pathologies, three ResNet-18 networks serve as binary specialist verifiers. ResNet addresses performance degradation in deep models through identity shortcut mappings:
$$y = F(x, \{W_i\}) + x$$
where $x$ and $y$ are the input and output vectors of the residual unit, and $F$ represents the residual function to be learned. In this system, each specialist network focuses its representational capacity entirely on discriminating one disease state from normal lung parenchyma.

3. Mathematical Formulation of Grad-CAM:
To compute the discriminative localization map for class $c$, Grad-CAM calculates the gradient of the class score $y^c$ (before softmax) with respect to feature map activations $A^k$ of the convolutional layer:
$$\alpha_k^c = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{i} \sum_{j} \frac{\partial y^c}{\partial A_{i,j}^k}$$
where $Z$ is the spatial area of the feature map (height $\times$ width), and $i, j$ index spatial coordinates. The neuron importance weights $\alpha_k^c$ capture the relative importance of feature map $k$ for target class $c$. The class-discriminative heatmap $L_{\text{Grad-CAM}}^c$ is computed by taking a weighted combination of forward activation maps followed by a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU):
$$L_{\text{Grad-CAM}}^c = \text{ReLU}\left(\sum_k \alpha_k^c A^k\right)$$
The application of ReLU ensures that the visualization highlights only features that positively correlate with the target pathological concept.

4.3 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
The software implementation is structured into two primary environments: the Python deep learning server and the React client application.

1. Model Ingestion and Preprocessing Pipeline:
Upon startup, the FastAPI server preloads the four PyTorch model checkpoints into persistent memory via `RuntimeModel` wrappers, securing thread-safe inference through Python threading locks. When an incoming image is processed, the system executes:
```python
# Radiograph normalization protocol
img_array = np.array(image.convert("L")) # Convert to single-channel greyscale
img_array = xrv.datasets.normalize(img_array, 255) # Scale to [-1024, 1024]
img_tensor = torch.from_numpy(img_array).unsqueeze(0).unsqueeze(0).float()
img_tensor = xrv.datasets.XRayCenterCrop()(img_tensor)
img_tensor = xrv.datasets.XRayResizer(224)(img_tensor)
```
This guarantees that patient radiographs from different scanning devices conform strictly to the spatial distribution expected by the DenseNet backbone.

2. Cloud Storage and AWS S3 Integration:
To archive incoming scans without blocking the user interface, the S3 service utilizes asynchronous buffer handling. The system dynamically reads environment configurations:
- If `FLOCI_ENDPOINT_URL` is set (e.g., `http://localhost:4566`), Boto3 redirects API calls to the local GraalVM-powered Floci emulator.
- If running on AWS Cloud, the endpoint URL defaults to native AWS endpoints, leveraging IAM role credentials.
The module creates the S3 bucket if absent, generates a timestamped, UUID-tagged key, and uploads the image with explicit metadata.

3. Frontend State and Visualization Pipeline:
The React 19 application maintains synchronized reactive states across file uploading, S3 storage verification, model prediction dictionaries, and Grad-CAM opacity levels. A specialized HTML5 Canvas overlay dynamically alpha-blends the raw radiograph preview with the base64 Grad-CAM heatmap according to the user's opacity slider (0% to 100%).


4.4 DATASET COLLECTION AND DESCRIPTIONS
The training, validation, and evaluation pipelines utilized the comprehensive multimodal Chest X-Ray (COVID-19 & Pneumonia & Tuberculosis) dataset sourced from Kaggle. The dataset aggregates validated, peer-reviewed clinical scans from the Italian Society of Medical and Interventional Radiology (SIRM), the GitHub COVID-19 image collection, the Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center pneumonia database, and the Montgomery County / Shenzhen Tuberculosis screening cohorts.

The dataset distribution comprises four balanced, highly curated categories:
1. COVID-19: Frontal radiographs depicting bilateral, peripheral ground-glass opacities, multi-focal consolidations, and acute respiratory distress features.
2. Normal: Healthy thoracic examinations demonstrating clear lung fields, sharp costophrenic angles, normal cardiothoracic ratio, and absence of consolidation.
3. Pneumonia: Bacterial and viral pneumonia cases presenting dense lobar consolidations, bronchograms, or diffuse interstitial infiltrates.

4.5 PSEUDOCODE AND CODE SNIPPETS

ALGORITHM 4.1: End-to-End Radiograph Screening and Cloud Archiving
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INPUT:  Raw image file F uploaded from frontend client
OUTPUT: Clinical JSON payload containing Predictions P, S3 Metadata S, and Grad-CAM Map G

1:  Function ProcessChestXRay(F):
2:      bytes <- ReadStream(F)
3:      If ImageType(bytes) is invalid Then Return Error(400, "Invalid image format")
4:      
5:      // Step 1: Cloud Object Archiving
6:      s3_key <- GenerateUniqueKey(F.filename, CurrentTimestamp(), UUID())
7:      S <- S3_PutObject(Bucket="chest-xray-images", Key=s3_key, Body=bytes)
8:      
9:      // Step 2: Quality & Anatomical Verification
10:     img <- DecodeImage(bytes)
11:     val_result <- VerifyChestRadiograph(img)
12:     If val_result.accepted == False Then
13:         Return Error(422, "Anatomical check failed", S3_Info=S)
14:     End If
15:     
16:     // Step 3: Deep Feature Preprocessing
17:     tensor <- PreprocessXRay(img) // Normalized to [-1024, 1024], resized to 224x224
18:     
19:     // Step 4: Multi-Model Inference
20:     logits, activations <- ForwardPassWithHooks(DenseNet121, tensor)
21:     probs <- Softmax(logits)
22:     core_pred <- ArgMax(probs)
23:     
24:     For each specialist in [COVID_ResNet, Pneumonia_ResNet, TB_ResNet] Do:
25:         spec_probs[specialist] <- Softmax(specialist.Forward(tensor))
26:     End For
27:     
28:     // Step 5: Explainability Heatmap Generation
29:     grads <- ComputeBackwardGradients(logits[core_pred], activations)
30:     alpha <- GlobalAveragePooling(grads)
31:     cam <- ReLU(SumOverChannels(alpha * activations))
32:     G <- OverlayColormap(cam, img, colormap=cv2.COLORMAP_JET)
33:     
34:     // Step 6: Consensus Arbitration
35:     decision <- ReconcileEnsemble(core_pred, probs, spec_probs)
36:     
37:     Return Response(Summary=decision, Models=probs, Heatmap=G, S3=S)
38: End Function

2. Grad-CAM Pathological Visualizations:
Visual examination of generated heatmaps confirms that the DenseNet-121 model focuses attention directly on physiologically verified pathological hallmarks:
- COVID-19 Scans: Grad-CAM generates hot activation clusters (red/yellow regions) across the peripheral, basilar regions of both lung lobes, matching the classic bilateral peripheral ground-glass opacities documented in COVID-19 literature.
- Pneumonia Scans: Heatmaps sharply localize unilateral lobar consolidations and dense peribronchial thickening, particularly in the lower right and left lung segments.

5.4 INTERPRETATION AND ANALYSIS
The experimental outcomes reveal several vital clinical insights:
1. Outstanding Infectious Disease Sensitivity: The system demonstrated near-perfect recall for the three major communicable diseases: 99.06% for COVID-19 (only 1 false negative out of 106 cases), 98.21% for Pneumonia (only 7 misses out of 390 cases), and 100.0% for Tuberculosis (0 misses out of 41 cases). In a clinical triage environment, false negatives carry catastrophic consequences—discharging an active tuberculosis or severe viral pneumonia patient risks death and widespread contagion. The proposed model prioritizes high recall, ensuring that virtually all infected patients are flagged for immediate isolation and confirmatory microbiological testing.
2. Understanding Normal Class Over-calling (Conservative Safety Bias): Of the 234 truly normal cases, 50 were classified as pneumonia. This accounts for the lower sensitivity of the Normal class (77.78%). Clinically, this behavior represents a "conservative screening bias." Chest X-rays from normal patients frequently display prominent broncho-vascular markings, mild cardiac enlargement, or slight patient rotation that mimic early viral interstitial infiltration. From a safety perspective, over-calling a healthy scan as "suspect pneumonia" results merely in secondary clinical review, whereas under-calling pneumonia as normal could prove fatal.
3. Decoupling Diagnostic Confusion via Multi-Model Arbitration: The incorporation of the three specialist ResNet-18 classifiers proved essential in resolving borderline ambiguities. When the primary DenseNet-121 model produced borderline confidence (60% to 80%), the specialist binary network provided a decisive confirming or refuting signal. If signals diverged, the system triggered an "Uncertain Result / Manual Review Needed" status rather than issuing an unjustified diagnosis.
4. Seamless Cloud Storage Utility: During load testing, image archival to S3 executed in an average of 145 ms via local Floci emulation and 380 ms across AWS Cloud native endpoints, successfully preserving diagnostic audit trails with zero disruption to the user experience.

6.1 CONCLUSION
This research project successfully engineered, validated, and deployed an automated, explainable, and cloud-integrated deep learning screening support system for pulmonary disease classification from chest radiographs. By coupling a domain-pretrained TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 backbone with three dedicated ResNet-18 specialist classifiers, the architecture overcomes the traditional trade-off between broad multi-class differential capability and specialized disease sensitivity.

Across an independent clinical test dataset of 771 patients, the proposed system demonstrated an overall classification accuracy of 92.22% and a validation accuracy of 94.74%. Most crucially for clinical safety, the system demonstrated extraordinary sensitivity across all targeted infectious diseases—detecting 99.06% of COVID-19 cases, 98.21% of Pneumonia cases, and 100.0% of active Tuberculosis cases.

The integration of Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) transformed the traditionally opaque neural network into an interpretable diagnostic tool, empowering radiologists and attending physicians to visually authenticate that the model's classifications originate from genuine anatomical lesions. Furthermore, the incorporation of AWS S3 cloud archiving—supported by the open-source Floci emulator for local development—establishes a scalable, cloud-native paradigm that bridges the gap between theoretical machine learning research and practical, clinical healthcare deployment.


6.2 FUTURE WORK
While the current system demonstrates high diagnostic fidelity and operational robustness, several promising avenues for future research and engineering enhancements are identified:
1. Integration of Multimodal Electronic Health Records (EHR): Future iterations will incorporate clinical tabular metadata—including patient age, oxygen saturation ($SpO_2$), body temperature, white blood cell (WBC) counts, and smoking history—into a cross-attention multimodal transformer network alongside radiographic features to improve specificity in distinguishing bacterial from viral pneumonia.
2. Extension to 14 Thoracic Pathologies: Expanding the model's diagnostic taxonomy to encompass non-infectious thoracic conditions such as cardiomegaly, pneumothorax, pulmonary edema, pleural effusion, and atelectasis using CheXpert multi-label objective functions.
3. DICOM Metadata Extraction and Edge Optimization: Developing native DICOM PACS (Picture Archiving and Communication System) network connectors with automated window-leveling (Hounsfield Unit calibration), while quantizing model weights via TensorRT and ONNX Runtime to enable real-time inference on edge computing devices and mobile medical clinics.
4. Federated Learning for Multi-Hospital Training: Implementing privacy-preserving Federated Learning protocols to collaboratively train models across geographically dispersed medical institutions without centralizing sensitive patient radiograph data, thereby complying with HIPAA and GDPR data governance regulations.
